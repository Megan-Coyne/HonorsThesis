Using Extreme Value Theory to Predict Municipal Decline from Corporate Data

Honors Thesis by Megan Coyne

Project Summary

Financial crises can have devastating consequences, not just for individual companies, but for entire cities. When a city depends heavily on a specific industry, economic downturns in that sector can lead to widespread financial distress and, in extreme cases, municipal bankruptcy. This project seeks to develop a statistical model using Extreme Value Theory (EVT) to better predict when a city’s financial health might be at risk due to struggles within key industries. Extreme Value Theory is an area within statistics designed to analyze rare but significant events, such as economic crashes. Traditional financial models often assume that market fluctuations follow a normal pattern, which underestimates the likelihood of extreme downturns. EVT, on the other hand, focuses specifically on extreme events, making it a more effective tool for assessing financial risk. This research will begin by applying EVT to financial data from Detroit, a city that declared bankruptcy in 2013 after years of economic decline, substantially correlated with the automotive industry. By analyzing financial trends from Detroit’s major auto companies alongside municipal economic indicators, I aim to identify patterns that signal financial distress that the city level. Building on this case study, I will then develop a more general EVT-based model, or highly complex formula in more simple terms, that can be applied to other cities with similar economic structures. The ultimate goal of this project is to provide a framework for predicting municipal financial instability based on corporate sector trends, and with even more in-depth research beyond the undergraduate level, potentially help policymakers and economists better anticipate and mitigate economic downturns before they reach a crisis point.

Project Abstract

Extreme Value Theory (EVT) provides a rigorous statistical framework for modeling rare and extreme events, such as financial crises leading to bankruptcy. Traditional financial models, even those incorporating extreme events, often assume Gaussian distributions and rely heavily on historical data, making them better suited for individual corporations than for entire municipalities. Detroit, the largest U.S. city to declare bankruptcy, provides a compelling case study due to its strong economic dependence on the automotive industry. This study applies EVT to the financial time series of Detroit’s three major automotive companies using the Peaks Over Threshold method and the Generalized Pareto Distribution. We examine how these data fit alternative distributions, including the Weibull distribution and the Marshall-Olkin transformation, and integrate multiple financial metrics such as stock returns, asset ratios, debt ratios, and stock prices to develop a custom statistical distribution for predicting municipal decline. To evaluate the predictive power of this model, we compare it with Detroit’s Economic Conditions Index, performing cross-correlation analysis and Granger causality tests to assess whether extreme corporate financial events, as identified through EVT, serve as significant indicators of municipal financial distress. This work provides a quantitative foundation for understanding the link between corporate instability and large-scale economic downturns and highlights the automotive industry’s central role in Detroit’s financial collapse. The framework could be extended to other U.S. cities with economies heavily dependent on a single industry, such as steel in Pittsburgh or technology in Silicon Valley, offering a tool to anticipate large-scale municipal financial crises.
