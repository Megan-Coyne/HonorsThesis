Overall, the stock return distributions, particularly for Ford and General Motors, display volatility clustering, heavy tails, and pronounced extreme movements during crisis periods, making them well suited for extreme value modeling. In contrast, the Detroit Economic Conditions Index shows smoother cyclical behavior and lower tail intensity, serving as a stable macroeconomic benchmark. These patterns support applying extreme value techniques to firm level returns while using the ECI as a comparative measure of broader economic conditions.

Fitting the "Best" Distribution

After conducting an initial examination of the data, the next step was to determine which distribution is most appropriate for modeling extreme values. In the honors thesis proposal, the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distribution was proposed as the primary model. However, this assumption needed to be tested rather than accepted at face value.

Several candidate distributions were explored to verify whether the GEV was truly the best choice. Many alternatives failed to adequately capture tail behavior or extreme spikes in the data. While fitting the data, using absolute maximum monthly returns and testing log transformations, the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) stood out. It effectively captured the heaviest tails, accommodated skewness, and provided more stable estimates for extreme losses and gains, making it the most suitable choice for modeling Detroit's stock extremes.

Ultimately, two distributions emerged as serious contenders: the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distribution and the Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD). The GPD, in particular, demonstrated strong performance in modeling threshold exceedances and provided a compelling alternative framework for extreme value analysis.

GEV (Generalized Extreme Value)

The GEV distribution models the maxima of datasets and captures extreme values effectively over block maxima. It is useful for understanding peak behavior but may be less flexible for threshold exceedances.

GPD (Generalized Pareto)

The GPD focuses on modeling threshold exceedances and the tail of the distribution. It provides flexibility in extreme value analysis, particularly when dealing with data above a certain threshold, and performed well for this dataset.

After evaluating both the GEV and GPD models across all three automakers, the GPD emerged as the stronger choice. While the GEV produced inconsistent p-values that occasionally fell below acceptable thresholds, the GPD returned consistently higher p-values across Ford, General Motors, and Stellantis/Chrysler, indicating a more reliable fit to the tail behavior of each return series.

With a well-supported distribution selected, the analysis can now move forward with confidence. The GPD provides the statistical foundation needed to estimate tail risk, calculate return levels, and build a robust extreme value model of Detroit's automotive stock returns.
